## Figure 1. Temporal buffering and phase–velocity division of labor

Conceptual sequence linking the PAYOFF-B1 timescale benchmark to the ecological
breakdown of local movement–timing substitution. In a moving front, timing
changes phase offset whereas movement speed controls mismatch drift, so bounded
timing can buy time without replacing spatial transport. Explicit landscapes
add finite capacity, route geometry and coordination constraints. Natural tests
then reject both one universal speed rule and the registered prediction that
stronger timing responsiveness should weaken later movement-speed dependence.
Direct systems decompose the remaining phase-control routes. The industrial
mule-deer Aikens test remains a preregistered within-taxon perturbation rather
than an additional cross-taxon replication.

## Figure 2. Finite temporal buffering and spatial re-entry

Frozen synthetic moving-landscape results. Increasing phenological capacity
expands the sampled persistence frontier, but frontier strategies remain
migration-dominant. In the canonical two-dimensional zigzag landscape at high
timing capacity, phenology-only tracking at forcing velocities 0.04–0.05 becomes
mixed by 0.06, and second-wall crossing reappears and increases at stronger
forcing. Phenological capacity also reduces the sampled zigzag route-growth
penalty. Model velocities and penalty magnitudes are properties of the declared
synthetic design, not empirical climate thresholds.

## Figure 3. Partner dependence blocks coordinated tracking reallocation

Frozen one-step coordination gate in the two-dimensional interacting landscape.
The matched resident `(m,h)=(0.2,0)` has joint low-density growth approximately
−0.840, whereas the adjacent coordinated strategy `(0.2,0.2)` has growth
approximately +0.255, a coordinated gain of +1.095. The same timing step made
unilaterally has gain approximately −5.946 because of transient partner
mismatch. In the declared fine positive-interaction grid, 22/24 cells contain a
coordination barrier and 21/24 convert a local-extinction endpoint to
coordinated persistence. These counts describe the sampled synthetic grid and
are not natural prevalence estimates.

## Figure 4. Broad natural data reject a universal speed rule and simple temporal substitution

(A) Registered Stage-1 reanalysis of 5,816 observations from 55 migratory bird
species. The flexible raw-mismatch minimum occurs near speed ratio 0.405 at
median directional alignment. After species-by-cell phase centering, the point
minimum moves to approximately 1.043 at median alignment and 1.397 under perfect
alignment, but coefficient-draw uncertainty is broad. (B) Species-level
curvature and vertex diagnostics remain heterogeneous. (C) Fresh chronological
holdout test: timing responsiveness estimated from 2002–2009 did not flatten
the 2010–2017 mismatch-versus-speed curve among 39 species and 3,268 holdout
observations. The registered quadratic interaction was +0.035 ± 0.029
(p=0.225), opposite to the predicted negative direction, so the substitution
test failed in direction. The timing-responsiveness main effect was negative in
the same model but is secondary descriptive evidence rather than the registered
primary result.

## Figure 5. Real migration systems transform phase error through different architectures

Segment-scale phase-retention estimates for mule deer, highlighted
barnacle-goose transitions and Eurasian wigeon, with independent environmental
reconstructions where available. Negative lambda retains its overshoot sign.
Raw coefficients share an estimator form but refer to different ecological
intervals and are not ranked as directly comparable biological rates. Secondary
whole-path retained-memory summaries make interval scale explicit: mule-deer
whole-migration retention is approximately 0.107, whereas a typical seven-
transition wigeon sequence retains approximately 0.133 under POWER and 0.231
under ERA5; the conservative SIMEX sensitivity permits substantially greater
retained memory. The panel supports a shared response language, not a universal
lambda.

## Figure 6. Environmental information, phase retention and actuation are distinct

(A) Stable barnacle-goose transitions occupy different combinations of
environmental innovation and segment-scale phase retention. (B) Across all eight
registered near/far definitions in the industrial-mule-deer perturbation,
movement-control permeability is lower in the large-development population;
the stronger prediction of additional temporal deterioration is not supported.
(C) The preregistered fixed-24 h Aikens phase-retention contrast is displayed as
`UNOPENED` in the PREOUTCOME figure set and is populated only from the
registered result JSON after authenticated environmental reconstruction.
