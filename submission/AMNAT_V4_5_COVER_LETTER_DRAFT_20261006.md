# Cover letter draft — The American Naturalist

Dear Editors,

Please consider our manuscript, **“Seasonal tracking depends on information
access and opportunities for correction,”** as a Major Article in *The American
Naturalist*.

Seasonal organisms face a problem that is often described with a single word,
predictability, but that problem contains several distinct biological stages.
An environment can be forecastable from spatial or temporal structure without
providing information that an organism can actually access at the relevant
decision time. Even accessible information can alter realized timing only while
useful responses remain available, and residual timing error can then be
corrected—or retained—after commitment. Our manuscript develops this sequence
as a common framework for environmental forecastability, organismal information
access, retained actionability and downstream correction.

The empirical analysis began with a prospectively specified test in a
multi-species migratory-bird dataset. Contrary to the registered degradation
prediction, detrended source–destination spring correlation increased strongly
between study periods. We retained that outcome as the primary registered
result rather than redefining success after outcome opening. Subsequent,
explicitly posthoc diagnostics showed why the correlation result required a
more careful biological interpretation. Destination spring variability
increased, while reconstructed nonlocal environmental structure gained strong
cross-validated predictive value. Yet the realized source environmental event
was not consistently available before the estimated migration front reached the
mapped source stage, and target green-up advanced substantially while
population arrival timing changed little. Route-level gains in analyst
forecastability also did not produce bird-specific improvement beyond
structural nulls.

These results motivate an important distinction: analyst forecastability is not
organismal information. We therefore separate an ideal-observer environmental
forecast layer from organismal access and retained correction opportunity. A
restricted same-system bird analysis shows that population-level relative
timing transforms across migration stages, but measurement-error auditing
prevents us from interpreting those stagewise coefficients as individual
feedback gains. Independent public-source-data reanalysis of Red Desert mule
deer provides the individual-level natural anchor: early and late animals alter
movement rate and stopover use in opposite directions and strongly contract
signed phase variation during migration. We explicitly treat this compensation
phenomenon as prior work rather than a new discovery.

We believe the manuscript is well suited to *The American Naturalist* because
its contribution is a general ecological architecture rather than a
taxon-specific correlation. It links information ecology, movement and
phenological tracking while making testable distinctions among what the
environment predicts, what organisms can know, what they can still change and
how residual error is corrected. The strongest prospective prediction is that
cue responsiveness should depend jointly on organismally accessible
decision-value and remaining actionability across ordered stages of a seasonal
trajectory.

The manuscript is deliberately conservative about causal scope. It does not
claim that birds used the reconstructed environmental predictor, that climate
change caused the two-window contrasts, or that the mule-deer mechanism explains
the bird pattern. Preregistered, posthoc, prior-art and prospective evidence
classes are identified separately throughout the manuscript and Supporting
Information.
