# Oikos Significance Statement

Existing work already shows that phenological mismatch can be buffered and that organisms can respond to environmental change across both space and time. Our manuscript asks a narrower question: what can a small endpoint mismatch actually tell us about the response producing it?

An exact local controller shows that movement- and timing-mediated correction can generate the same mismatch dynamics through a shared restoring gain. Low mismatch therefore does not identify whether the tracking burden is being carried in space, in seasonal timing, or by both. Explicit landscapes then show where this hidden compensation becomes constrained: timing provides a finite bypass of movement and route costs, after which spatial tracking re-enters. Interacting partners add a distinct failure mode because the reallocation needed to maintain tracking can be beneficial jointly but deleterious to either partner moving first.

The general ecological contribution is therefore not another demonstration that organisms shift in space and time, nor the generic claim that mismatch can be buffered. It is a mechanistic account of why endpoint mismatch can conceal a changing tracking architecture, and of when that buffering must change or fail because of finite capacity, landscape geometry, or partner dependence. The manuscript also converts each qualitative mechanism into a measurable prediction with an explicit falsification condition.

Oikos is an appropriate outlet because the paper uses a synthetic model to
derive a general ecological inference problem and converts that mechanism into
explicitly falsifiable field and experimental predictions rather than treating
the model's dimensionless thresholds as natural constants.
