# PAYOFF-B tracking theory: prior-art and novelty boundary

Frozen literature audit: **2026-09-24**

Purpose: position `manuscript/PAYOFF_B_TRACKING_THEORY_V1.md` without claiming novelty for components that are already established.

## 1. What is already established

### Multiple climate-response axes are already recognized as related ways of realigning organisms with their niches

Muthukrishnan et al. (2025), *Chasing the Niche: Escaping Climate Change Threats in Place, Time, and Space* (*Global Change Biology*, DOI: 10.1111/gcb.70167), explicitly frames behavioural change, phenological change, and range shifts as related strategies for realigning experienced conditions with a species' niche.

**Boundary for PAYOFF-B:** do not claim that it is novel to place movement, timing, and other responses into one conceptual climate-tracking framework.

### Combined range and phenology shifts are already associated with better outcomes in empirical systems

Hällfors et al. (2021), *Combining range and phenology shifts offers a winning strategy for boreal Lepidoptera* (*Ecology Letters*, DOI: 10.1111/ele.13774), analysed 289 Lepidoptera and found the strongest positive population trends among the minority of species showing both northward range shifts and phenological advances.

Macgregor et al. (2019), *Climate-induced phenology shifts linked to range expansions in species with multiple reproductive cycles per year* (*Nature Communications* 10:4455), likewise links phenological response to abundance and range expansion in British Lepidoptera.

**Boundary for PAYOFF-B:** do not claim that mixed spatial + temporal response is a new empirical possibility, or that a combination can never outperform a single response axis.

### Range and phenology shifts can act jointly at community scales

Ramirez-Parada et al. (2025), *Shifts in Phenology and Species Ranges Synergistically Alter the Timing and Species Composition of the Flowering Season* (*Global Change Biology*, DOI: 10.1111/gcb.70607), models both response types across thousands of plant species and shows that range and phenology shifts alter different aspects of flowering-season structure and can act synergistically.

**Boundary for PAYOFF-B:** novelty is not the statement that range and phenology shifts interact.

### Moving-habitat and climate-velocity models are established

Harsch et al. (2017), *Moving forward: insights and applications of moving-habitat models for climate change ecology* (*Journal of Ecology*, DOI: 10.1111/1365-2745.12724), reviews spatially explicit moving-habitat models and their use for dispersal, multi-dimensional space, population structure, and interspecific interactions.

Modern range-shift syntheses likewise emphasize that observed redistribution often differs from simple climate expectations because of habitat structure and species interactions.

**Boundary for PAYOFF-B:** do not claim novelty for using a moving environmental envelope, spatial redistribution, habitat barriers, or interspecific effects in a climate-tracking model.

### Dispersal can alter phenological evolution in spatially structured populations

Pontarp et al. (2015), *Adaptation of timing of life history traits and population dynamic responses to climate change in spatially structured populations* (*Evolutionary Ecology* 29:565–579, DOI: 10.1007/s10682-015-9759-6), models evolution of reproductive timing in two habitats linked by dispersal. Dispersal changes mismatch, population responses, competitive outcomes, and long-term evolutionary dynamics.

**Boundary for PAYOFF-B:** do not claim that coupling dispersal, timing evolution, population dynamics, and ecological interactions is itself unprecedented.

The sharper distinction is that PAYOFF-B makes **movement effort and timing effort themselves alternative heritable tracking axes**, then asks whether a coordinated change in axis allocation is accessible under unilateral evolution.

### Spatial eco-evolutionary climate models already combine dispersal, evolution and species interactions

Åkesson et al. (2021), *The importance of species interactions in eco-evolutionary community dynamics under climate change* (*Nature Communications* 12:4759, DOI: 10.1038/s41467-021-24977-x), develops a spatially explicit framework that integrates dispersal, trait evolution and interspecific interactions under climate change. It also shows that combinations of dispersal, evolutionary capacity and interactions can have non-additive effects on persistence.

A particularly close 2026 comparison is Shirani, Miller & Freeman (2026), *Coevolution of species' range borders: Interactions between interspecific competition, gene flow, and matching habitat choice* (*Evolution Letters* 10:427–440, DOI: 10.1093/evlett/qrag022). Their two-species theory includes interspecific competition and an adaptive, phenotype-dependent dispersal rule that moves individuals toward habitats reducing phenotype–environment mismatch.

**Boundary for PAYOFF-B:** do not claim novelty for combining dispersal, evolution and species interactions under climate change, or for allowing interacting species to evolve directed mismatch-reducing movement. The remaining distinction is narrower: PAYOFF-B gives each partner two alternative climate-tracking axes (space and timing), then tests whether changing the *allocation between those axes* is accessible through unilateral improving substitutions. The canonical gate compares the same adjacent tracking-axis change made jointly versus by one partner alone.

### Phenological mismatch between interacting species is a mature literature

Visser & Gienapp (2019), *Evolutionary and demographic consequences of phenological mismatches* (*Nature Ecology & Evolution* 3:879–885, DOI: 10.1038/s41559-019-0880-8), reviews the evolutionary and demographic consequences of phenological mismatch.

Kharouba & Wolkovich (2020), *Disconnects between ecological theory and data in phenological mismatch research* (*Nature Climate Change* 10:406–415, DOI: 10.1038/s41558-020-0752-x), emphasizes the need for explicit baselines, fitness links, experiments, and null models.

Johansson et al. (2015), *The eco-evolutionary consequences of interspecific phenological asynchrony – a theoretical perspective* (*Oikos*, DOI: 10.1111/oik.01909), synthesizes life-history, population-dynamic, and evolutionary-game approaches to asynchrony.

Rafferty, CaraDonna & Bronstein (2015), *Phenological shifts and the fate of mutualisms* (*Oikos* 124:14–21, DOI: 10.1111/oik.01523), reviews how differential phenological shifts can alter mutualistic interactions.

**Boundary for PAYOFF-B:** do not claim novelty for climate-driven mismatch, loss of temporal overlap, or demographic effects of asynchrony.

### Evolution of interacting phenologies under climate change has already been modelled

Gilman et al. (2012), *Evolution of plant–pollinator mutualisms in response to climate change* (*Evolutionary Applications* 5:2–16, DOI: 10.1111/j.1752-4571.2011.00202.x), explicitly models evolution and population dynamics of interacting plant and pollinator phenologies under climate change.

Memmott et al. (2007), *Global warming and the disruption of plant–pollinator interactions* (*Ecology Letters*, DOI: 10.1111/j.1461-0248.2007.01061.x), demonstrates that climate-driven phenological shifts can disrupt mutualistic networks.

**Boundary for PAYOFF-B:** do not claim that coevolving timing or interaction loss under climate change is new.

## 2. What PAYOFF-B can claim more narrowly

The candidate contribution is the conjunction of the following estimand separations.

### A. Exact local substitution null -> explicit breakdown

PAYOFF-B starts from an exact controller identity

```text
e_(t+1) = [1-(q_m+q_h)] e_t + r
K = q_m + q_h
```

so movement-mediated and timing-mediated correction are mathematically interchangeable at the local mismatch level before costs, bounds, and spatial mechanics are restored.

The explicit landscape then asks **which ingredients break that null**. The frozen result is a transition from local substitutability to forcing-dependent complementarity.

This is a sharper claim than simply saying that multiple response axes can interact.

### B. Finite temporal bypass -> spatial re-entry

PAYOFF-B gives phenology a finite shift capacity and movement an explicit spatial realization. In the frozen 2D route design, timing can temporarily avoid corridor traversal, but migration re-enters as environmental forcing increases.

The candidate contribution is therefore not “phenology and range shifts combine,” but:

> a temporal response can act as a finite bypass of geometric movement demand, with a model-explicit point at which spatial tracking must re-enter.

Any novelty claim should remain mechanism-level rather than asserting a universal threshold.

### C. Coordinated value != unilateral accessibility

The strongest candidate novelty is the direct local coordination gate.

The model compares:

```text
both partners shift tracking axis together
versus
one partner makes the same one-step change while the other remains fixed.
```

In the canonical 2D cell, the coordinated step is strongly beneficial while either unilateral step is strongly deleterious.

The key claim is therefore:

> climate adaptation can be blocked not because a viable tracking strategy is absent, but because the viable strategy is inaccessible through the sequence of individually favourable changes available to interacting species.

This is more specific than generic phenological mismatch, coevolutionary lag, or competitive exclusion.

### D. Synchronization can change ecological sign with forcing

The partner-asymmetry experiment separates two regimes using the same matching interaction:

```text
moderate forcing:
interaction synchronizes different intrinsic responses and all pairs persist

strong forcing:
interaction synchronizes pairs onto a local migration-only attractor and all pairs fail
```

The claim is not that synchrony is generally bad. It is that the **same coupling mechanism can switch from beneficial alignment to maladaptive lock because the synchronized attractor moves outside the persistence envelope**.

### E. Accessibility != demographic visibility

The independent higher-replication demographic rerun removes the unstable large-cell-effect story.

The retained result is:

```text
mechanistic coordination barrier
does not imply
large demographic effect everywhere.
```

Population consequences are most visible near persistence transitions.

This gives a direct explanation for why a real evolutionary constraint can be hard to detect from abundance or persistence data in safe regimes.

### F. Drift crossing != drift rescue

The finite-N layer explicitly separates the probability of crossing a coordination barrier from the long-run payoff after crossing and exploring the wider strategy landscape.

The frozen result licenses:

> small populations can cross a deterministic barrier more often while still having lower long-run mean joint growth.

This is a useful refinement of generic evolutionary-rescue language.

## 3. Manuscript framing to use

The safest high-level framing is:

> Existing work shows that organisms can respond to climate change in space and time, that combinations of range and phenology shifts can be beneficial, and that dispersal and species interactions can alter phenological evolution. PAYOFF-B asks a different question: when these response axes are jointly available to interacting species, is the best combined tracking architecture reachable by unilateral adaptive change? The model shows that local space-time substitutability can break into finite temporal bypass, spatial re-entry, and a coevolutionary coordination gate in which a jointly persistent adjacent strategy is individually inaccessible.

Avoid:

- “first model combining dispersal and phenology”;
- “first model of phenological mismatch under climate change”;
- “first demonstration that range and phenology shifts can be complementary”;
- “first moving-habitat model with species interactions”;
- “interactions prevent climate adaptation” as a general statement;
- “drift rescues locked populations.”

Prefer:

- “separates adaptive capacity from unilateral accessibility”;
- “uses exact local substitutability as a null and identifies how explicit landscape mechanics break it”;
- “shows a one-step coevolutionary coordination gate between alternative tracking axes”;
- “predicts finite temporal bypass followed by spatial re-entry”;
- “separates barrier existence, demographic visibility, stochastic crossing, and long-run payoff.”

## 5. Current novelty ceiling

The literature audit supports a **narrow but defensible** novelty statement:

> The new object is not space-time climate response itself. It is the evolutionary accessibility of switching between spatial and temporal tracking architectures when interacting partners must remain matched. The exact local substitution null, finite temporal bypass, one-step coordination gate, demographic visibility window, and finite-N crossing/load separation are the manuscript's distinctive chain.

This boundary should be revised only if a closer published model is found that explicitly allows both partners to evolve allocation between spatial and temporal tracking axes and demonstrates the same unilateral coordination barrier.
